Summary

You are looking for an MVP that is simple, robust and extensible, to test a WhatsApp concierge service in Marrakech with real travellers. A guest scans a partner's QR code, describes what they want in their own words, the AI structures the request, the system asks the relevant suppliers on WhatsApp, an operator supervises, and the guest gets one or more options through to a booking. Around that loop: a light back-office, human takeover at any moment, partner attribution, and numbers you can actually read.

This document answers the version 4 specification point by point — recommended architecture, why this is written as code rather than assembled in an automation tool, the data model, what goes in V1 and what waits for phase 2, an eight-week schedule to a live pilot, a price per lot, what the pilot has to sell to pay for itself, and the monthly running cost.

Four recommendations

  1. Build it as one product, not two halves. The pieces depend on each other — a request is worthless without a supplier to ask, supplier replies are worthless without a back-office to read them, and none of it means anything without attribution and measurement. V1 is the complete loop, errands included, each part in its simplest robust form. Phase 2 holds only what the pilot's own data should decide.
  2. Two WhatsApp numbers, not one. One for guests, one for suppliers. It separates the two audiences at the level that matters: quality rating, sending limits, display name and template sets are independent, so a supplier who blocks or reports the supplier number can never affect the number your guests write to.
  3. Make the AI engine swappable from day one. Google, Anthropic, OpenAI and OpenRouter are all supported in V1, with the active model chosen in the back-office. No lock-in, and you can compare quality, latency and cost on your own conversations during the pilot.
  4. Measure the pilot inside the product. The KPI dashboard moves into V1. A pilot exists to produce a decision, and that decision needs conversion, response times and attribution visible on a screen your team opens every morning — not extracted from a database by a developer.

How I read the project

The product is a conversational intermediary between three populations: travellers, service suppliers (restaurants, transport, activities, wellness) and referring partners (riads, hotels, shops) identified by QR code. The value of the pilot is not the chatbot. It is the complete loop — request, solicitation, replies, offer, booking — with trustworthy data at every step.

Three principles run through the whole proposal, following your specification:

  • The AI never decides a commercial fact. Availability, prices and supplier characteristics come only from the catalogue and from recorded supplier replies.
  • A human stays in the loop: supervision, takeover on the same number, and the final choice left to the operator whenever they want it.
  • The backend is independent of the channel. WhatsApp is the first channel; a web app or a mobile app can be plugged into the same engine later without a rewrite.

One local detail worth stating: your suppliers in Marrakech will answer in French, in Darija, sometimes in a mix, and often in a single line with no punctuation. Free-text replies are interpreted by the AI and flagged to the operator when they are ambiguous, rather than silently guessed at.

4.2 Why a provider rather than going direct to Meta

Meta's Cloud API can be used directly, and it is slightly cheaper on paper. In practice the friction sits in business verification, number registration, template approvals and quality-rating incidents — and that is exactly where a pilot loses two weeks. 360dialog handles that layer for a fixed fee per number and passes Meta's message fees through at cost. Because it is the same Cloud API underneath, the integration is a base URL and a token: moving to Meta direct, or to another provider, later is a configuration change rather than a rebuild.

4.3 The journey of one request

Taking the example above: a rooftop table for four tonight, around 300 dh per person.

  1. The guest scans the partner's QR code. It points at a short link on your own domain, which records the scan and then redirects to WhatsApp — see 4.5 below. By the time the guest types anything, the partner has already been credited.
  2. The AI extracts category, date, time, party size, budget, zone and preferences, and asks one targeted question when something essential is missing.
  3. Through tool calling, the AI creates the request and queries the catalogue. The system filters active suppliers in the category by zone, standing, price band and capacity. This step is a database query, not a judgement call by the model.
  4. The system sends the shortlisted suppliers a structured WhatsApp message from the supplier number — an approved template with the request and reply buttons. The request moves to sent, then waiting. The operator sees the request and the suppliers contacted, and can add, remove or call anyone directly.
  5. Supplier replies are captured: buttons for available or unavailable, free text interpreted by the AI and confirmed by the operator when ambiguous. Every reply is timestamped.
  6. The operator, or the system depending on the rule you choose, selects the options to present. The AI writes them to the guest using recorded data only.
  7. The guest chooses. The request moves to booked or cancelled, and the transaction is recorded with amounts and commissions for information. Payment happens directly with the supplier.

4.4 Two numbers: what it buys and what it costs

4.5 How a scan is actually attributed

The obvious way to track a QR code is to point it straight at a WhatsApp link carrying a pre-filled message with the partner's code in it. It is also fragile: the guest can clear that text before sending, and when they do, the lead arrives with no source at all. On a pilot whose entire purpose is to prove which partners produce business, losing attribution is losing the result.

So the QR codes point at a short link on your own domain instead — go.yourdomain.com/elfenn — and that link does three things in a few milliseconds:

  1. Records the scan server-side before anything else: partner, timestamp, device and language, plus a dedupe window so the same phone scanning twice in a minute counts once. You get scan counts per partner from day one, whether or not the guest ever opens a conversation. A direct WhatsApp link can never give you that number.
  2. Redirects to WhatsApp with a temporary redirect, not a permanent one, so every future scan keeps reaching your server and the destination can be changed later — a second number, a landing page, a web chat — without reprinting a single QR code.
  3. Ties the conversation back to the scan. The pre-filled message carries a short code, which handles the normal case. When a guest clears it, the first message from an unknown number is matched to the most recent unclaimed scan inside a short window, which resolves nearly all of the rest. If two scans from different partners collide in that window, the request is flagged in the back-office as attribution to confirm and an operator decides. The system never invents an attribution silently — a wrong partner credit is worse than a missing one.

The same mechanism is what makes a second acquisition channel free to add later: any link, from anywhere, gets its own code and lands in the same dashboard next to the partner riads.

6.2 The request state machine

A request is in exactly one state, and only the permitted transitions can happen. Each transition is timestamped, which gives you response and handling times for free.

to handle → sent → waiting → available / unavailable → offer sent → booked / cancelled → completed
  • Unavailable returns to waiting if other suppliers are still being asked, or moves to cancelled if no option exists.
  • Cancelled is reachable from any active state, with a reason attached (customer, supplier, operator).
  • Completed is reached after the service is confirmed, or after a configurable delay with no feedback.

A conversation follows the same logic with two states, AI and human. In human mode the AI never replies. Returning to AI mode is an explicit operator action, and the AI receives a summary of what happened while it was out.

7.1 Phase 2, after the pilot

Four features deliberately left out of V1, because each one is a decision the pilot data should make rather than an assumption I build in now:

  • Automatic supplier ranking and pre-selection — ordering suppliers on price, response time, reliability and acceptance rate, and contacting the best ones without operator input. Every field it needs is already recorded in V1; what is missing is the evidence for how to weight them, which is exactly what the pilot produces.
  • Automatic follow-ups — chasing a supplier who has not replied, a guest who has not chosen, and notifying operators. The right delay before a chase is a question the pilot answers.
  • Additional languages — Arabic, Spanish, German, added to the extraction and the replies. V1 launches in French and English; the pilot tells you which language you are actually losing guests in.
  • Automated commission rules — calculating and tracking what each partner is owed, instead of recording the amounts for manual settlement.

Anything not listed in this document — mobile app, online payment, full CRM, advanced scoring, brand identity — is out of scope, in line with section 18 of your specification.

At that volume the build is repaid inside the first six weeks of the pilot and the service clears more each month than it cost to create, on a cost base that has barely moved: the servers are the same, the numbers are the same, and only message fees and model usage grow with the traffic. That gap between flat costs and growing revenue is the whole argument for building the machine properly once.

Two levers move these numbers more than anything technical. The first is margin: at 20% instead of 15%, the pilot-target case repays the build in about six months rather than nine. The second is the mix — a 1,200 MAD private transfer or a day trip costs the same to process as a 300 MAD dinner and carries four times the margin, which argues for loading the catalogue with high-value categories early rather than proving the concept on restaurant tables alone.

The second city is where the economics change. Everything above pays for building the machine once. Opening Agadir, Casablanca or Essaouira afterwards is a new partner list and a new supplier catalogue loaded through the back-office — no new development, no second server, no second WhatsApp number. The marginal cost of a request in city two is the same five to twelve cents of model usage plus Meta's message fee. That is the real reason to spend eight weeks building this properly rather than eight weeks assembling something that only works in Marrakech.

And if the quiet scenario happens. That is not a failure, it is an answer — bought for roughly the cost of two months of a junior salary, and the dashboard will tell you precisely where the loop broke: partners not handing out the card, guests not converting, or suppliers not answering. Each of those has a different fix, and all three are cheaper than building the product twice.

Personal data

The system holds guests' phone numbers and conversations, and a large share of your guests will be European. This is a short section because the pilot is designed to hold as little as possible, but it is not a section to skip.

  • What is stored: WhatsApp number, first name if the guest gives one, conversation history, requests, supplier replies and transaction amounts. No card numbers, no identity documents, no payment data — the pilot never handles a payment.
  • Where: a server in Germany, inside the EU, with encrypted transport, daily backups and access limited to named operator accounts, each action attributable to a person.
  • For how long: a configurable retention period, set at delivery. Twelve months for conversations is a sensible pilot default, with a documented procedure to delete an individual guest's data on request — a European guest can ask, and you will need to be able to do it.
  • Who else touches it: Meta and 360dialog carry the messages, and the active model provider receives conversation text to generate a reply. Under the standard business terms of the providers proposed here, that content is not used to train their models. All of them are listed as subprocessors in the delivery documentation.
  • Moroccan law: processing personal data requires a declaration to the CNDP under law 09-08. That filing is yours to make as the data controller; I provide the technical description it needs — data categories, purpose, retention, hosting location and the subprocessor list — as part of the documentation.
  • Suppliers: their consent to receive WhatsApp business messages is collected when they join the catalogue, and recorded against their record.

This describes how the system is built, not legal advice. Your counsel should confirm the filing and the retention period before the pilot opens to real guests.
